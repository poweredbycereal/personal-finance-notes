## Why Most People Lose Money In The Market

1. We think we can time the market.
    - We cannot. The rises and falls are too unpredictable.
1. We believe we can pick individual stocks.
    - Most of us cannot. Only a few (e.g. Buffett) can do so reliably.
1. We believe we can pick winning mutual fund managers. In a 2023 study by Dimensional Fund Advisors, 83% of equity funds failed to outperform the unmanaged index.
1. We focus on the foam.
    - The rises and falls of the market in the short-term are often just noise relative to the long-term.

## The Big Ugly Event

Events like the Stock Market Crash of 1929 are a scary thought to investors. LJ Collins notes, "Should you have been unlucky enough to invested at the peak, your portfolio wouldn't have fully recovered until the mid-1950s, twenty-six years later. Yikes. That's enough to try the toughest investor."

However, he makes the following points:
1. It would have taken an investor of exceptionally bad luck--one who had bought their entire portfolio precisely at the 1929 peak--to have borne the full weight of the crash. Suppose instead you had invested in 1926-27. Looking at our chart, this is about halfway up on the climb to the peak (...) Certainly, they were destined to lose all their gains, and yet ten years later, had they held on, they'd be back in positive territory.
1. Someone who started investing after the crash would be in great shape for gains.
1. Someone who lost 90% of a million dollars would be left with $100,000, but the Great Depression being a deflationary event, they would have gained buying power.
1. The Big Ugly Event has happened only once in the history of the Dow, dating back to 1896.
1. 2008 cam close the edge of the abyss, but things ended up working out.

Deflationary depression like that of 1929 and hyperinflation are both economic disasters that can destroy wealth on a major scale.

Funds like VTSAX are pretty good inflation hedges because their values rise with inflation.

## Keeping It Simple: Considerations And Tools


The more complex an investment, the less likely it is to be profitable.

Index funds outperform actively managed funds in large part simply because actively managed funds require expensive active managers.

You'll want to consider the following:
1. In what stage of your investing life are you? The wealth accumulation stage or the wealth preservation stage? Or perhaps a blend of the two?
1. What level of risk do you find acceptable?
1. Is your investment horizon long-term or short-term?

Safety is a bit of an illusion. There is no risk-free investment. Once you begin to accumulate wealth, risk is a fact of life. You can't avoid it, you only get to choose what kind. If you invest in stocks, you'll likely outpace inflation and build wealth, but you'll have to endure a volatile ride.

The wealth accumulation stage comes while you are working, saving, and adding money to your investments. The wealth preservation stage comes once your earned income slows or ends. Your investments are then left to grow and/or are called upon to provide income for you.

F-You Money is critical. If you don't yet have yours, I suggest you start building it now. It is never too late to start. Be persistent. Life is uncertain. Remember that nothing money can buy is more important than your fiscal freedom.

Don't be quick to think short-term. Most of are, or should be, long-term investors.

Three tools:
1. Stocks: VTSAX (Vanguard Total Stock Market Index Fund). Stocks provide the best returns over time and serve as our inflation hedge. This is our core wealth-building tool.
1. Bonds: VBTLX (Vanguard Total Bond Market Index Fund). Bonds provide income, tend to smooth out the rough ride of stocks, and serve as our deflation hedge.
1. Cash: Cash is good to have around to cover routine expenses and to meet emergencies. Cash is also king during times of deflation. The more prices drop, the more your cash can buy. What when prices rise (inflation), its value steadily erodes. In these days of low interest rates, idle cash doesn't have much earning potential. I suggest you keep as little as possible on hand, consistent with your needs and comfort level. Typically, money market funds pay slightly more than bank savings accounts, but not always. And while money markets are considered to be extremely safe, they don't offer the FDIC insurance (up to $250,000) found with bank accounts.

## Index Funds Are Really Just For Lazy People, Right?

No.

Reasons Collins believes that many resist the idea of indexing:
1. It is a challenge for smart people to accept that they cannot outperform an index that simply buys everything.
1. To buy the index is to accept the market's "average' return. People have trouble accepting the idea of themselves or anything in their life as average. But in this context, the word *average* is mostly understood. Rather than meaning index fund returns are at the midpoint, the word *average* here means the combined performance of all the stocks in an index. Over periods of 15-30 years, the index will outperform 83% or more of actively managed funds. That means buying a total stock market index fund guarantees you'll be in the highest performance tier.
1. The financial media is filled with stories of individuals and professionals who have outperformed the index for a year or two or three. Or of those who, in very rare cases (like Buffett), have outperformed over time. But investing is a long-term game.
1. People underestimate the drag of costs to investing. Paying fund and/or advisor fees of 1%-2% seems low, especially in a good year. Over time, these expenses are significant.
1. People want quick results, excitement, and bragging rights.
1. There is a huge business dedicated to selling advice and brokering trades to people who can be persuaded they have what it takes to outperform. Money managers, mutual fund companies, financial advisors, stock analysts, newsletters, blogs, and brokers all want their hands in your pocket. Billions are at stake, and the drumbeat marketing the idea of outperformance is relentless. In short, we are brainwashed.
